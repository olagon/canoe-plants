# Research notes

Notes from the fact checking pass. Each line records a fact that was cut because it could not be confirmed, a seed fact from the brief that was corrected, or a claim that was kept but flagged because sources disagree. Plant pages list the sources that were actually read.

## Kalo, ʻuala, ʻulu, niu, maiʻa

- KALO kept but flagged. The seed said fewer than 100 varieties survive. CTAHR (cho-2007) gives exact numbers instead, 84 distinct kinds collected 1928 to 1935, 70 left by 1992, 10 more lost to pigs after Hurricane Iniki. Used those.
- KALO sources disagree. CTAHR says peak kalo land was more than 20,000 acres. Hawaiʻi ʻUlu Cooperative says about 35,000 acres. Used the CTAHR figure. The under 400 acres figure is from 2007 and may be dated.
- KALO sources disagree on the poi bowl rule. Bishop Museum says arguing would offend Hāloa. Hawaiʻi ʻUlu Cooperative says poi stands for the presence of Kāne. Fact text follows Bishop Museum and the moʻolelo notes the Kāne kinolau.
- KALO the 28,000 year old starch claim rests on one source, cho-2007 citing Loy and colleagues 1992. Surprising fact with only one source read. Consider softening or cutting.
- KALO the line that voyagers carried huli is an inference from how kalo is propagated. No source read states it directly.
- ʻUALA debated and presented as a debate. Human transport from South America (roullier-2013) versus natural dispersal (munoz-rodriguez-2018). Ioannidis 2020 was read through the Smithsonian summary and search abstracts, not the full paper. Roullier 2013 on pnas.org returned 403, so its claims were read through the Denham 2013 PNAS commentary and the California Academy of Sciences summary.
- ʻUALA changed. Seed said 3 to 6 months to harvest. The grower guide says 4 to 6 months, used that. A Ka Wai Ola line saying ʻuala is ready within a month or so was not used.
- ʻUALA changed. Seed said the Kohala field system covered tens of square kilometers. Verified figure is more than 60 square kilometers (marshall-2017).
- ʻUALA cut. Lei of ʻuala vines worn by mothers (Bishop lists it without explaining the purpose) and ʻuala beer.
- ʻULU kept. 150 to 200 fruits a year, seedless root shoot propagation, sap for caulk and birdlime, NTBG largest collection all confirmed in ragone-2006 and NTBG pages. The Bounty fact was confirmed from search results of the Northwestern University release, the page itself was not opened.
- ʻULU flagged. The kaluʻulu size (10 square miles) and yield (33 million pounds a year) come only from the Hawaiʻi ʻUlu Cooperative and are worded as an estimate. West Hawaii Today describes the grove as about half a mile wide from above Kailua to Hōnaunau.
- NIU cut. The common claim that a coconut can float 110 days and still sprout traces to a 1941 Bishop Museum Occasional Paper that I could not open as text, so no number of days is given.
- NIU softened. The seed said only one eye is soft enough for the sprout. NTBG only confirms that the sprout comes out through one of the three pores, so that is all the fact says.
- NIU flagged. The Niuolahiki story details (the chant, the boy climbing, the tree stretching to Kahiki) come from Beckwith, Hawaiian Mythology, chapter 34, which I could only read as search excerpts because sacred-texts.com and Ulukau blocked automated fetches. ʻAha Pūnana Leo confirms the outline. Tellings differ on who chants (Hina or the grandparents), so the text says his elders.
- NIU the no nails fact is supported by the Polynesian Voyaging Society plant list (niu for sennit) and Bishop Museum (husk fiber for lashing). The exact words no nails are mine. Hawaiians had no metal before 1778.
- NIU grow notes come from University of Florida extension, not a Hawaiʻi source.
- MAIʻA cut. Banana stalks as spear practice targets, not confirmed. Canoe rollers confirmed by Bishop Museum.
- MAIʻA sources disagree on variety counts. Seed said around 50, not confirmed. A UH Kapiʻolani library guide says over 70 (no citation given), Bishop Museum says nearly 20 survive, Kahanu Garden holds more than 30 including Pacific kinds. Presented as uncertain.
- MAIʻA sources differ on which bananas women could eat. Bishop Museum lists pōpōʻulu, iholena and kaualau. The Pukui and Elbert dictionary names iholena and pōpōʻulu as examples.
- MAIʻA flagged. Kāne and Kanaloa farming bananas at Alakahi in Waipiʻo is from Beckwith, Hawaiian Mythology, read as a search excerpt only.
- MAIʻA the today section relies on a 1990 CTAHR fact sheet for which bananas are most grown. Still broadly true but dated.
- ALL five plants. Abbott 1992 and Krauss 1993 are listed as references on the Bishop Museum database pages, but no page tied a specific claim to them, so they are not cited. handy-handy-1972 is listed for kalo only because cho-2007 cites it for the ʻohana and ʻohā link.

## Kō, ʻawa, kukui, noni, wauke

- KUKUI cut: the seed claim that each nut burned about 15 minutes and worked as a clock. William Ellis (1826) and CTAHR (both quoted at Images of Old Hawaiʻi) say 2 to 3 minutes per nut. Only Wikipedia gives 15 minutes. The fact now uses the Ellis figure and says modern writers differ. The suggested hook about the nut being a clock was replaced.
- KUKUI flagged: kinolau. Canoeplants.com, Plant Pono and Maui Nō Ka ʻOi (citing Abbott) say Kamapuaʻa. The Bishop Museum database lists Lono. Field is set to Kamapuaʻa and the moʻolelo text states the disagreement. Cut: burial of Lanikāula in the grove (could not open the Hana Hou article), archaeological dates for candlenut use in Timor (Wikipedia only), years until a kukui tree bears nuts (no source found).
- KŌ cut or softened: exact count of Hawaiian varieties. Sources range from nearly 40 (canoeplants.com) to 50 to 60 surviving cultivars (Lincoln quoted in Hana Hou 2012) to dozens (UH Press). Text says dozens. Variety names kō kea, ʻainakea and uahiapele were not used because I could not read a trusted description of them. Settler arrival date of 600 AD from one magazine was not used. The 2025 Cell sugarcane paper was read only through the CIRAD press release.
- KŌ sourcing note: Kōloa 1835 and the December 2016 final harvest were confirmed from search summaries of the Wikipedia Old Sugar Mill of Koloa page, Maui Now and Star-Advertiser, not from a full page read. Low risk but worth a second look.
- ʻAWA notes: the Lebot 1999 finding (13 Hawaiian cultivars, probably from one introduced variety) was read as summarized in Johnston and Rogers 2006, because the journal page is paywalled. Variety spelling mōʻī follows the meaning royal given by Titcomb (she prints moi). Please have a Hawaiian speaker confirm. Kinolau of Kāne rests on the UH Kahoʻiwai akua list only. Cut: the most prized ʻawa sprouting in trees (passage was cut off in my copy).
- NONI cut: the Kamapuaʻa chant taunting Pele about noni (found only on travel and blog sites, could not read Beckwith), the nickname vomit fruit (not in any source read), seeds floating for months (CTAHR only confirms an air chamber), any dollar value for the noni industry, and kinolau of Kū (canoeplants.com only, so the field is left empty). The ʻōlelo noʻeau is given in English only because I could not confirm the diacritics of the Hawaiian text.
- WAUKE cut: the claim that Hawaiian kapa was the finest in the Pacific (opinion, only secondary pages), scented kapa, and kapa as strictly the expert work of women (not stated in a source I read). Flagged: harvest age. Sources give 6 to 10 months (canoeplants.com), 18 months (Images of Old Hawaiʻi) and about every 2 years (Peñailillo 2016), so the grow note says usually within 2 years. Two versions of the Maikohā story exist (Nuʻuanu in Westervelt and Alameida, Kaupō in Beckwith as seen in a search summary) and the text says so. Halekulani Living says voyagers brought wauke seeds, which conflicts with the genetic work showing clonal spread, so that detail was not used.

## ʻOhe, ʻōlena, ʻawapuhi, uhi, piʻa, hoi

- ʻOhe: CUT the seed claim that this bamboo flowers only once every few decades. NTBG says viable seed about every 30 years and Plant Pono says about every 20 years, neither with a citation, so no number was used.
- ʻOhe: KEPT the umbilical cord fact but tied it to its primary source. David Malo (Hawaiian Antiquities, chapter 35) describes it for the birth of a high chief, so the text says that and does not claim it for every birth.
- ʻOhe: CUT the story that Hina planted a grove of ʻohe from Tahiti on Maui. Found only on canoeplants.com with no source. Also CUT the claim that the stems are too weak for building, since NTBG and Bishop Museum list house frames as a use.
- ʻOhe: The small-leafed bamboo of Kāne prayer in Malo comes from a circumcision ceremony. The site text quotes the phrase without describing that ceremony, for the age of the audience. Kinolau of Kāne is confirmed by the Bishop Museum ethnobotany database.
- ʻOhe: could not reach Kew Plants of the World Online to confirm a native range, so the text makes no claim about where the species first grew wild.
- ʻŌlena: CUT the seed hook The only spice in the canoe. Could not verify that Hawaiians used it as a food spice, and ʻawapuhi leaves also flavored food. Hook replaced.
- ʻŌlena: CUT seed claims that it was rubbed on skin and dripped in noses as stated. Kept only what Bishop Museum (citing Abbott 1992 and Chun 1994) and Pukui and Elbert support, which is earache and nasal or throat remedies.
- ʻŌlena: the kī leaf used as the sprinkler in pī kai comes from magazine and UH Hilo descriptions of present practice, not from Malo. The text words it as the practice as often described today.
- ʻŌlena: dormancy months differ by source (UH Hilo says December to April, canoeplants.com says September to March), so the text only says winter.
- ʻAwapuhi: kinolau of Kāne is stated by NTBG (and canoeplants.com). The Bishop Museum database leaves the kino lau field empty. Worded as is said to be. FLAGGED as less firmly sourced than the ʻohe kinolau.
- ʻAwapuhi: CUT any claim that the rhizome dyed kapa. Only scenting kapa is confirmed.
- ʻAwapuhi: the shampoo brand is described but not named in the copy. Source is the company history page (Paul Mitchell, farm founded 1983 at Paʻauilo).
- Uhi: the seed said tubers reach tens of pounds. Used 5 to 10 kg typical and up to 60 kg from the Useful Tropical Plants database citing Tindall 1983. The horse could carry only two line is from the Niihau Cultural Heritage Foundation and is worded as something people once said.
- Uhi: CUT the claim that Niʻihau was called Yam Island by sailors. Could not confirm it on a page I could read. Also CUT vitamin C and scurvy claims.
- Uhi: the seed said Hawaiians grew it in drier valleys and in mounds. Bishop Museum (citing Wagner 1990) says cultivated in dry to mesic areas. Mounds appear only as a grow tip, not as a history claim. No recorded moʻolelo found, so the moʻolelo slot holds the documented history of the 1810 Kamehameha yam field and says so plainly.
- Uhi: 44,000 year old yam starch on stone tools in Papua New Guinea (Sharif 2020) was found but left out of the facts as too easy to misread. The never found wild, always cloned fact is from the same paper.
- Piʻa: sources DISAGREE and the text says so. Malo calls it a good and satisfying food. Krauss 1993 (via Bishop Museum) and St. John and Jendrusch 1980 list it as famine food. Probably never cultivated is from Kent 1986 only.
- Piʻa: no moʻolelo, saying or kinolau found in any source. The moʻolelo slot honestly says so and describes the recorded custom. Grow notes are thin because no Hawaiʻi growing guide exists. CUT Wikipedia details (3 pound tubers a meter deep, horseshoe shaped bulbils) as not confirmed elsewhere.
- Hoi: the three sayings are from ʻŌlelo Noʻeau by Pukui as quoted in the Bishop Museum database. Only Ulu ka hoi is given in Hawaiian because I could not confirm the exact diacritics of the other two. The trussel2.com concordance was unreachable.
- Hoi: CUT the Hawaiʻi Pacific Weed Risk Assessment score (reported as 13, high risk) because the PIER and HEAR pages would not load. Florida facts are from USDA. USDA pages give two different Florida arrival dates (1905 on the NISIC profile, about 1909 implied by the ARS story). Used 1905.
- Hoi: uses are split into steps of one detox process from Malo to reach the minimum count honestly. Aerial tubers are tagged as stem for piʻa and hoi because the schema has no bulbil part.
- General: Abbott 1992, Krauss 1993 and Handy and Handy 1972 are cited only where the Bishop Museum database attributes a claim to them. Malo 1903 was read directly in the archive.org full text.

## Pia, ʻape, kamani, milo, ipu

- CUT (ʻape): seed fact that leaves were held up as umbrellas in Hawaiʻi. Only Wikipedia (general tropics) and an unsourced garden blurb say this. No trusted Hawaiian source found.
- CORRECTED (ʻape): the seed said ʻape was planted near houses to keep away evil. The Pukui and Elbert dictionary says the opposite detail. It was planted by gates and fences to ward off spirits and NOT near homes for fear residents would get sick. Text follows the dictionary.
- CUT (ʻape): claim that ʻape was brought over 1,500 years ago (NTBG, canoeplants.com). This conflicts with the settlement dates now accepted (around 1000 to 1200 CE), so no date is given.
- CUT (ʻape): ʻape as an ingredient in a dye for ipu gourds (NTBG and Plant Pono do not say which plant part, so it could not be placed in uses), domesticated in the Philippines, and the antifungal protein alocasin. Single weak sources.
- CUT (pia): hats plaited from pia flower stalks, and stems woven into mats. Only PFAF and sources about other islands. Not confirmed for Hawaiʻi.
- CUT (pia): relative of the bat flower, the toxin name taccalin, the claim that pia starch is more nutritious than cornstarch, and Ke Ola magazine's elevation limit of 830 feet. Single or weak sources.
- NOTE (pia): no recorded moʻolelo was found. The moʻolelo field is an honest word history from the Pukui and Elbert dictionary (pia as the general word for starch, pia Hawaiʻi) and says so in the text. The harvest figure of 8 to 10 months is my estimate from the spring sprout and winter harvest cycle described by Plant Pono and Bornhorst, so a reviewer may prefer to drop the number.
- CUT (kamani): flowers begin to open at 3 to 4 am (NTBG only, one source for a surprising claim). Also cut the Samoan belief that every part is a virulent poison.
- NOTE (kamani): the seed floating fact is supported by the title and topic of Hanaoka and colleagues 2014 and by Wikipedia. I could not open the Friday and Okano 2006 agroforestry profile because the PDF blocks text extraction, so nothing is cited from it. The grow line about cracking the shell and slow growth is common horticultural guidance that a reviewer should confirm against that profile.
- NOTE (kamani): the line that Makalapua honors Queen Liliʻuokalani is common knowledge about the mele. The Bishop database confirms only that the kamani blossom line appears in Makalapua (Elbert and Mahoe 1970).
- FLAGGED AS DEBATED (milo): status. Bishop Museum database lists it as 'indigenous?', CTAHR says probably indigenous but maybe Polynesian introduced, Maui Nui Botanical Gardens says undetermined, Plant Pono says native. Text says probably brought by voyagers but scientists are not fully sure.
- CUT (milo): medicine use. The seed table lists medicine, but the Bishop database documents no Hawaiian medicinal use, and canoeplants.com only says the tree 'yields medicine' with no detail. The medicine tag was left off.
- CUT (milo): the milo house post from a Lānaʻi archaeological site (Native Plants Hawaiʻi page now returns 404, seen only in a search snippet) and rongorongo tablets of Rapa Nui (not about Hawaiʻi).
- NOTE (milo): sources describe the flower color change differently (purple in the seed, purplish pink on canoeplants.com, orange at Maui Nui Botanical Gardens and Plant Pono). The text says orange or purplish pink. The claim that milo items were only for chiefs comes from canoeplants.com alone and is worded as 'one source says'.
- FLAGGED AS DEBATED (ipu): the dual Asian and American ancestry of Polynesian gourds (Clarke 2006) is presented as a hint that needs more testing, as the authors themselves say. Kistler 2014 showed American gourds came from Africa by ocean drift.
- NOTE (ipu): planting time. The Bishop database says plant at the beginning of the rainy season. canoeplants.com and Duff 2019 say after or at the end of the rainy season. The grow text avoids naming a month and says to time it so fruit ripens in dry weather.
- CUT (ipu): more than 100 varieties grown in old Hawaiʻi, the ipu hōkiokio nose whistle being unique to Hawaiʻi, gourds used to chase away sharks, and the story of the pot bellied planter. Single sources or not found.
- NOTE (ipu): the Niʻihau inside out dye method is cited to Wikipedia and matches several gourd artist pages. canoeplants.com supports that this decoration was done only in Hawaiʻi. The RMG record notes the masked paddlers scene is not mentioned in the voyage journals.

## ʻŌhiʻa ʻai, hau, ʻauhuhu, kī, kou

- GENERAL. Abbott 1992, Krauss 1993 and Handy and Handy 1972 were not read directly, so no fact cites them. The Bishop Museum ethnobotany database entries for all five plants list them as its references (for example hau cites Abbott 1992 p101, Handy 1972 p233, Krauss 1993 p71). Native Plants Hawaiʻi (nativeplants.hawaii.edu) was down during research and the DLNR ʻōhiʻa ʻai page returned 403, so neither is cited.
- OHIA-AI cut. Kinolau of Kū and links to Pele and Kāne. canoeplants.com and NTBG say this, but in a passage that blends ʻōhiʻa ʻai with ʻōhiʻa lehua. Left kinolau empty and explained why in the moʻolelo text.
- OHIA-AI cut. Seed claims that Waipiʻo fills with the smell in fruiting season and that fruit must be eaten within a few days. No source found. Replaced with the verified detail that the skin is thin and soft. Also cut the saying about the month Hinaiaʻeleʻele (only canoeplants.com, could not check ʻŌlelo Noʻeau).
- OHIA-AI kept. Hula altar (kuahu) offering rests on the Bishop database, which cites Emerson 1909. Emerson itself could not be fetched. Wikipedia says the fruit has one seed, canoeplants.com says 1 to 2. Text says single big seed per Little and Skolmen.
- HAU debated. Sources split on status. Bishop database says indigenous with a question mark, Little and Skolmen 1989 and Maui Nui Botanical Gardens say indigenous, Plant Pono says most agree it is both indigenous and a Polynesian introduction, canoeplants.com says Polynesian introduction. The Burney 2001 cave paper does not mention hau at all. Text follows the brief and says probably carried, not fully sure.
- HAU debated. Fire making. Little and Skolmen say a hard olomea stick was rubbed on a grooved soft hau base. canoeplants.com says a pointed hau stick was rubbed in harder wood such as kukui. The fact follows Little and Skolmen and the uses line stays neutral.
- HAU cut. Legend that hau is a sister of Hina turned into a tree, and the Tahitian grandchild of heaven and earth line (only canoeplants.com). Used the kapu shoreline custom from Thrum 1907 instead, read in the original text.
- AUHUHU changed. The seed said the chemical is rotenone. The Bishop database and Pukui and Elbert era sources name tephrosin, a rotenoid in the same family as rotenone. One Wikipedia page says the plant is reported to contain both. Text says tephrosin, in the rotenone family. Primitive Ways (citing Abbott 1992) adds that the Hawaiian fish drugs worked more like an anesthetic than rotenone because fish recovered.
- AUHUHU cut. Claims that families caught ʻoʻopu by damming a stream pool, and that divers sealed underwater caves, appeared only in search summaries that I could not trace to a page. Also no moʻolelo or kinolau is recorded, so the moʻolelo field describes the hola practice and the dictionary example instead.
- KI cut or softened. William Stevenson first distilling ʻōkolehao in the 1790s (Wikipedia only), only kahuna and aliʻi wearing ti at the neck (Wikipedia only), the rain cape name kui lāʻī (one web source), and any baking time for the root. Kī is described as sacred to Lono and Laka (NTBG, canoeplants.com, Kapiʻolani CC) but no source calls it a kinolau, so kinolau is empty.
- KI kept as belief. The ti leaf float test for moʻo is modern folklore (Kapiʻolani CC garden page, Dartmouth Folklore Archive) and is labeled as a belief people still tell. The hukilau fact leans on Parker 1922 and Place Names via wehewehe. Pukui and Elbert gloss lau there as ropes, so the text says ropes hung with ti leaves.
- KI sterility. Hinkle 2007 abstract (PubMed) confirms the Eastern Polynesian green form is sterile through greenhouse crosses and low genetic variation. The idea that Polynesians selected the sterile form for bigger roots is speculation (Wikipedia) and was left out.
- KOU verified in the original paper. Burney et al. 2001 p632 to 633 says eight AMS dates on kou fruits range from 5300 to 5945 cal yr BP, that most authors had considered it a Polynesian introduction, that Sinclair 1885 thought it indigenous, that Cordia appears only as traces in pollen (weak pollen producer), and that hala also predates people. The seed said fossil pollen and charcoal. The real evidence is mainly preserved fruits, so the text says fruits.
- KOU kept with care. Moth damage in the 1800s rests on Wikipedia plus Little and Skolmen (many trees were destroyed by moths). The moth name Ethmia nigroapicella is left out of the copy. The shade tree and kapa beating tradition is from canoeplants.com with Little and Skolmen and the Bishop database confirming kou was planted as a house shade tree. Cut the claim that Kou, the old name of Honolulu harbor, comes from the tree, because Place Names of Hawaiʻi says it was named for a person. Cut a Polynesian legend that kou was one of the first trees created (one source, no detail). Cut use for ʻea (thrush) because the plant part is not stated.
